# Internal evidence: constraints before candidate selection

Research date: 2026-10-16. This note separates what the text says from what an authorship hypothesis adds. It does not identify the author or assign candidate probabilities.

## Hebrews 2:3 and Paul

[Hebrews 2:1–4](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/2) places the narrator among those for whom the message was confirmed by earlier hearers. That is a meaningful contrast with Paul's insistence in [Galatians 1:11–17](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/1) on receiving his gospel through revelation rather than human instruction. Taken autobiographically, Hebrews presents someone downstream from the initial proclamation, making direct Pauline composition less natural.

This is not a logical disproof on its own. Hebrews uses an exhortatory collective “we”; Paul also says he transmitted what he received in [1 Corinthians 15:3–11](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15). Receiving tradition and claiming a revelatory commission need not be contradictory. The defensible argument concerns differing authorial self-presentation, reinforced by other evidence, rather than an impossible sentence for Paul to write. Nor does “second generation” by itself fix a calendar date.

## Hebrews 13:22–25: network evidence with assumptions

[Hebrews 13:18–25](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/13) contains personal requests, a blessing, Timothy, greetings from people associated with Italy, and a grace formula. If these are the composer's authentic personal circumstances, they locate the writer in a network familiar with Timothy. They do not uniquely identify Paul or any particular colleague.

The [Greek of 13:24](https://www.greekbible.com/hebrews/13/24) describes people “from Italy”; that wording alone does not establish whether the writer is in Italy or with Italians elsewhere. Likewise, the notice that Timothy has been released does not independently provide a dated imprisonment. Reconstructing an itinerary from these verses requires additional assumptions.

The epilogue's relation to the body is therefore a prior question. [Clare K. Rothschild's *Hebrews as Pseudepigraphon*](https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/book/hebrews-as-pseudepigraphon-9783161515248/) (2009) argues that an original epilogue imitates Pauline correspondence and constructs Pauline identity. This differs from proposing a later interpolation. Pauline influence, ordinary letter conventions, imitation, and separate composition must remain distinguishable explanations. The existence of Pauline-looking language does not itself choose among them.

## Hebrews 11:32: grammatical gender and narrator identity

In the [Greek text](https://biblehub.com/text/hebrews/11-32.htm), διηγούμενον modifies first-person με. The straightforward reading represents a masculine speaker. This is substantive evidence against identifying that speaker directly with Priscilla, not evidence about women's intellectual abilities.

[Ruth Hoppin](https://www.cbeinternational.org/resource/priscilla-and-plausibility/), “Priscilla and Plausibility,” argues for neuter/generic or conventional-language explanations and discusses a hypothetical textual alteration. These proposals are not an attested feminine reading. [Bridget Jack Jeffries](https://etsjets.org/wp-content/uploads/JETS_67.1_47-66_Jeffries.pdf), “Hebrews, She Wrote?”, *JETS* 67.1 (2024), 47–66, especially 61–65, challenges the grammatical parallels and identifies a useful methodological constraint: if the masculine voice belongs to an imagined public reader, other first-person statements may also be reader-oriented.

Apply the same narrator model across candidates. Treating 2:3 as strict biography to exclude Paul while treating 11:32 as a generic performance script to preserve Priscilla needs independent justification. Neither “certainly male composer” nor “gender irrelevant” follows automatically.

## Candidate fit is not identification

[Acts 18:24–28](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/18) presents Apollos as Alexandrian, eloquent, scripturally knowledgeable, and a Christian teacher. These features fit Hebrews' broad profile; they are not an independent writing sample. No secure comparison corpus for Apollos, Priscilla, or the apostolic Barnabas is supplied here. A classifier cannot identify any of them from biography alone.

In particular, the work conventionally called *Epistle of Barnabas* must not be silently labelled as authenticated prose by Paul's companion. Comparing it with Hebrews would be a comparison between literary works whose historical attribution requires separate argument. Likewise, similarity to Luke–Acts concerns its literary author; connecting that author to the historical Luke is another inference. “Unknown author” must remain an explicit possibility.

## Next experiment: epilogue sensitivity — not yet run

The companion computational analysis uses omission of all chapter 13 as a coarse sensitivity check. A narrower, preregistered follow-up should compare Hebrews through 12:29, through 13:17, through 13:19, and complete Hebrews; examine 13:20–25 separately against other letter closings.

Control for closing conventions, quotation, and passage length. Test whether apparent Pauline affinity is concentrated in the epilogue rather than assuming it is. Do not choose boundaries after seeing which candidate wins. The closing is too short for confident standalone attribution; its purpose here is to detect dependence of a result on a specific textual region. Even a positive result would not by itself distinguish imitation, genre switching, or multiple hands.
