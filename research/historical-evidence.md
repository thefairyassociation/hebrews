# Historical evidence for Hebrews: what the witnesses establish

Research note, 16 October 2026. **No attribution is established here.** The ancient testimonies below and the modern arguments cited are established scholarship. The contribution new to this repository is their separation into testable claims, source dependencies, and controls for computational comparisons. A report of an attribution is evidence that someone held that attribution, not direct evidence of the act of composition.

## 1. Origen: distinguish intellectual authorship from composition

In [*Letter to Africanus* §9](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0414.htm), Origen explicitly offers to defend Pauline authorship. In [*Against Celsus* 3.53](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04163.htm), he identifies the author of Hebrews 5:12–14 with Paul, who wrote 1 Corinthians. These primary passages make it unsafe to present him simply as an opponent of Pauline authorship.

The famous excerpt in [Eusebius, *Church History* 6.25.11–14](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm) distinguishes the epistle's Greek style from Paul's, assigns its thoughts to Paul, and leaves its literary hand uncertain. It reports earlier Clement and Luke attributions. Thus it does not straightforwardly exclude Pauline participation.

[Matthew J. Thomas, *New Testament Studies* 65.4 (2019), 598–609](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0028688519000274), argues from Origen's wider corpus that the uncertainty concerns a collaborator. That interpretation deserves consideration; it is not proof that the collaboration happened. Exact dating of the lost Hebrews homilies is disputed, so a supposed late reversal by Origen must not be assumed.

**Research implication:** distinguish direct Pauline composition, Pauline material shaped by another writer, and independent composition. Ordinary stylometry principally addresses the surviving wording.

## 2. Clement of Alexandria: a specific translation model

[Eusebius 6.14.2–4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm) preserves Clement's account: Paul wrote in Hebrew, Luke translated, and the resulting expression resembles Acts. Explanations are offered for the missing Pauline signature. The presbyter invoked in §4 is unnamed in that passage. Clement's lost work is mediated through Eusebius.

**Research implication:** a Hebrew original, Luke's involvement, and Pauline participation are separate propositions. Refuting one does not automatically refute the others. Stylistic resemblance to Acts is part of the ancient reasoning, not an independent confirmation to count again.

## 3. Tertullian: genuine testimony to a Barnabas attribution

In [*On Modesty* 20.1–5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0407.htm), Tertullian introduces Hebrews under Barnabas's name and quotes Hebrews 6:4–8. The quotation identifies canonical Hebrews, not the separate work called the *Epistle of Barnabas*. The [Latin, 20.2](https://www.tertullian.org/latin/de_pudicitia.htm), uses *Barnabae titulus ad Hebraeos*.

This is early-third-century attribution evidence, not a surviving signed copy. A manuscript heading or library label is a possible explanation of *titulus*; its existence and transmission history cannot be reconstructed from this sentence alone. Tertullian appeals to Hebrews in a polemic about post-baptismal repentance, which matters when evaluating his emphasis on its authority. The [Fribourg patristic catalogue](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/77/versions/728) gives 213–223; other chronologies differ, so an exact year would overstate certainty.

## 4. Clement of Rome: quotation can masquerade as authorial style

[Eusebius 3.38.1–3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm) says that 1 Clement borrows from Hebrews, then favours Clement as translator because the texts resemble each other. [1 Clement 36](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm) contains readily inspectable parallels to Hebrews 1.

**Research implication:** compare the Greek after masking direct overlaps and independently identified scriptural quotations. A high similarity score before those controls could reflect borrowing or common sources. Surviving similarity would still need comparison with genre and shared religious vocabulary. Literary dependence itself does not logically exclude common authorship, but it prevents treating every matching phrase as independent evidence for it.

## 5. P46: collection membership is observable; its cause is not

The [University of Michigan's manuscript description](https://apps.lib.umich.edu/online-exhibits/exhibits/show/from-trace-to-text--highlights/highlights-from-the-university/epistles-of-st--paul) records Hebrews immediately after Romans in P46. The [library dates it approximately to 200 CE](https://www.lib.umich.edu/about-us/news/old-world-making-news/).

This is material evidence for early association with the Pauline collection. Authorship, collaboration, an associate's work, or a collector's judgment can each produce such placement. Collection membership cannot discriminate between them without additional evidence; palaeographic dating is approximate.

## 6. Witness dependence, not a tally of votes

[Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* §5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm), explicitly attributes the Barnabas view to Tertullian. [Eusebius 3.3.5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm) reports Roman opposition to Pauline attribution. Neither passage licenses a numerical majority argument.

| Witness | Observable evidential channel | Dependence or limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Clement 36 | Literary parallels | Does not name Hebrews' composer |
| P46 | Physical collection placement | Establishes association, not its origin |
| Clement of Alexandria | Lost account quoted by Eusebius | Translation theory plus stylistic reasoning |
| Origen | Own writings; lost homilies quoted by Eusebius | Separate source of ideas from literary hand |
| Tertullian | Attribution accompanying a Hebrews quotation | Earlier attribution chain unspecified |
| Jerome §5 | Reports Tertullian's Barnabas attribution | Explicitly dependent, not another early witness |

## Candidate identifiability and the next historical test

A comparison with Paul, Luke–Acts, or 1 Clement can test relationships between surviving corpora. It cannot identify Barnabas, Apollos, or Priscilla without independently authenticated comparison material. A text bearing a candidate's name is not automatically a valid training sample. Retain an **unknown author** option and avoid converting nearest-neighbour rankings into probabilities of historical identity.

The strongest pending historical lead is the **transmission of the Latin Barnabas attribution**. E. A. de Boer's article, [*Vigiliae Christianae* 68.3 (2014), 243–263](https://brill.com/view/journals/vc/68/3/article-p243_1.xml?language=en), DOI [10.1163/15700720-12341168](https://doi.org/10.1163/15700720-12341168), was verified against Brill's publication record, abstract, and accessible references. The publisher's full text requires access. An [author-posted copy, p. 251](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275457471_Tertullian_on_Barnabas%27_Letter_to_the_Hebrews_in_De_pudicitia_201-5), supplies a lead to *Tractatus de libris sacrarum scripturarum* 10, generally attributed to Gregory of Elvira: Hebrews 13:15 is assigned to Barnabas. **Verification in the critical edition and independence from Tertullian remain pending.**

Do not treat the Claromontanus list's Barnabas entry as settled corroboration. [Kelsie G. Rodenbiker (2021), especially note 5](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0142064X211055647), explicitly contests identifying that entry with Hebrews and distinguishes the sixth-century manuscript from a hypothetical older list.

The next useful deliverable would be a small, edition-checked dossier of Latin quotations, attribution wording, and possible textual dependencies. Even a demonstrably independent attribution lineage would strengthen evidence for an early belief, not prove Barnabas wrote Hebrews.
